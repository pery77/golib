# The 3D engine

How GoLib's 3D will be built: the layers, the contract with the graphics card, the budgets measured for it and the conventions it keeps. The plan, its stages and the decisions behind it are in [roadmap.md](roadmap.md#m7-3d-planned).

> **Status: M7, being built.** Stage 0, a spike that measured what this design rests on, was done on 2026-10-20, and every number here comes from it. Stage 1 landed the same day: the contract below in both backends, the math, the camera, the basic meshes and the 3D pass, which [framework/README.md](../framework/README.md#3d) describes for games. Stage 2 followed: glTF models with their materials and textures, decoded by the browser on the web, markers from Blender, and automatic instancing and culling. Stage 3 then: each pass drawn in half floats and tonemapped over the 2D, physical materials, the sun's cascaded shadows, lamps, HDRI skies whose sun becomes the pass's, fog, bloom and FXAA. Stage 4: animation, posed in Go and bent on the graphics card. Stage 5: FBX files, read in Go into the same document as glTF ones. Stage 6, its part in Go: collisions against a level's triangles in a bounding volume hierarchy, a character controller, paths over a grid of walkable places, and positional sound. Rigid bodies and the rest are the next stages.

## Layers

```text
game       the 3D API, in package golib: small, with working defaults
golib      the engine, plain Go: math, scene, glTF reader, materials, renderer, animation, collisions
device     the GPU contract: objects made once, and commands each frame
  raylib   OpenGL 3.3, called directly through the pointers raylib's loader found
  web      WebGL 2: commands written into the 2D's command buffer, run by web.js
           and rigid bodies: Jolt, in golibnative (native/) through purego, or JoltPhysics.js in web.js
```

Rigid bodies are the one part not in Go. Package golib keeps every body's triangles in its own `PhysicsWorld` too, so rays, the character controller and the navigation mesh stay in Go and see rigid bodies, and Jolt gets a copy of the world's still and moved things, the moved ones as kinematic bodies, and a kinematic capsule for each character that moves, a tenth thinner than its controller. The contract passes bodies and their states as arrays of floats, one call for all of them, since purego's `SyscallN` passes no floats and every call into the page costs (see [native/README.md](../native/README.md)). A character pushes a body by impulses worked out in Go, as a person of 80 kg pushing with at most 3000 N, not by its capsule, which would push with no limit. On the desktop the simulation repeats exactly, as `golib shot` needs; the desktop and the browser run different builds of Jolt, so their bodies drift apart over seconds.

raylib keeps what it does today: the window, input, sound and the 2D. The 3D uses none of raylib's models, animations, shaders or `rlgl` drawing.

Light baked in Blender comes in as a lightmap, a Radiance `.hdr` beside the model, on its meshes' second texture coordinates, `TEXCOORD_1`, in the vertex layout `LayoutLightmapped`; a program compiled with `LIGHTMAPPED` reads it in place of the sky's and the ground's light. Light probes come in a JSON file beside it, as four spherical harmonics a probe in a data texture, which the lit program without `LIGHTMAPPED` blends from the eight around each pixel. The bake, its two passes and its units are in [roadmap.md](roadmap.md#m7-3d-planned), stage 7.

## The GPU contract

The contract is shaped like WebGL 2, which is OpenGL ES 3.0, and OpenGL 3.3 has everything it needs. It has two kinds of calls:

- **Objects, made rarely:** buffers, meshes (a vertex layout over a vertex buffer and a 32-bit index buffer), textures (8-bit and floating-point formats, sRGB, mipmaps), render targets with a depth attachment, and shader programs with their uniform locations and uniform blocks. These are direct calls on both backends.
- **Commands, every frame:** begin and end a 3D pass on a target, clear depth, use a program, set a uniform or a uniform block, bind a texture, set the depth, cull and blend state, draw a mesh, draw a mesh instanced. The desktop runs them at once; the web writes them into the command buffer the 2D already uses, so 2D and 3D stay in the order the game drew them.

About 30 functions in all, and a few more as the stages need them: shadow maps and work targets for stage 3, data textures for stage 4. Rules that come with it:

- **No questions to the graphics card while drawing.** In Chrome, `gl.getError` took 61 µs: a round trip to the GPU process. Reading anything back (errors, pixels, parameters) happens at load time or for a screenshot, never per frame.
- **Few calls per draw.** What changes once a frame (the camera, the lights) goes in uniform blocks set once; a draw sets its matrices and its material and draws. The engine instances repeated meshes by itself.
- **The 3D pass leaves the state as raylib expects it.** On the desktop it sends raylib's waiting 2D first, and afterwards turns depth testing off, culling and blending back on, and unbinds its program and vertex array, with texture unit 0 active.
- **The screen's target has depth on both backends.** raylib's render textures already have a depth buffer; the web backend's targets get one.

### Animation on the graphics card

A model's pose is worked out in Go, once a frame for each `Animator`: its animations sampled, blended and layered, each node placed, and each joint's matrix, where the joint is times where the mesh was bound to it. The vertices are bent on the graphics card:

- A skinned mesh has `device.LayoutSkinned`: each vertex carries its four joints and their weights after the lit layout's twelve floats.
- Every animated item of a pass has a block of texels in one data texture, RGBA in 32-bit floats, 1,024 texels a row: four texels for each joint's matrix, then its shape keys' weights, four to a texel. The texture is filled once a pass, from the command buffer on the web, before the shadows.
- Copies of one mesh with one material are still one instanced draw: their blocks lie one after another, and the vertex shader finds its own from `gl_InstanceID` and the block's size. Uniform arrays would have held about 60 matrices in WebGL; a texture holds as many as a crowd needs.
- A mesh's shape keys are a data texture of its own, two texels for each vertex of each, found from `gl_VertexID`; the vertex shader adds those whose weight isn't zero before skinning.
- The lit and shadow programs have a variant that bends, compiled the first time something animated is drawn; nothing else changes for what isn't animated.

Measured on the iMac above: 400 of Kenney's characters, 1,497 vertices each, walking at their own speeds, posed in 0.85 ms and issued with their shadows in about 1 ms more, at 60 frames a second.

### Why not rlgl

`rlgl` is raylib's layer over OpenGL, and raylib-go exposes it, but stage 0 found three reasons to go under it:

| | rlgl through raylib-go | OpenGL through `rlGetProcAddress` |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Cost of a call (libffi or purego) | 120 ns | 70 to 100 ns with `purego.SyscallN` (235 ns with `purego.RegisterFunc`, whose reflection costs more) |
| Indexed draws | `rlDrawVertexArrayElements` draws 16-bit indices only, so meshes over 65,535 vertices must be split | Any index type |
| Uploading pixels | `rlLoadTexture` isn't in the binding; only raylib's `Image` path, RGBA without sRGB | Any format, sRGB included |

Calling OpenGL directly also makes the desktop backend a near copy of the web one, call for call.

## Budgets

Measured on 2026-10-20 on an iMac (Intel, AMD Radeon Pro 5300, macOS's OpenGL 4.1), in a window without V-Sync, and in headless Chrome on the same machine, whose WebGL runs on the GPU through ANGLE and Metal. "Issue" is the time to send the frame's 3D; "finish" waits until the graphics card has drawn it too.

| Scene | Desktop: issue / finish | Web, command buffer: issue / finish | Web, direct calls: issue / finish |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Sponza: 103 draws, 262,267 triangles, 69 textures | 0.29 / 0.92 ms | 0.08 / 3.09 ms | 0.68 / 3.44 ms |
| 1,000 cubes, one draw each | 1.07 / 1.39 ms | 0.22 / 3.12 ms | 3.69 / 5.57 ms |
| 4,000 cubes, one draw each | 3.92 / 4.56 ms | 0.84 / 6.38 ms | 14.32 / 16.70 ms |
| 10,000 cubes, one draw each | 9.91 / 10.47 ms | 2.11 / 13.29 ms | 36.82 / 40.79 ms |
| 10,000 cubes, instanced | 0.27 / 0.62 ms | 0.34 / 2.77 ms | 0.37 / 2.42 ms |
| 100,000 cubes, instanced | 1.89 / 3.08 ms | 3.75 / 8.76 ms | 3.33 / 7.79 ms |

A call from Go into the page costs about 200 ns bare and 600 to 900 ns for a WebGL call with its arguments; writing a command into the buffer costs 65 ns. What it means:

- **About 1 µs a draw on both backends**, with the command buffer on the web. A frame of 60 a second spends at most 2 ms on draws, about 2,000 of them. More objects than that are instanced.
- **The web needs the command buffer.** Direct calls cost three times as much at 10,000 draws and leave no frame for the game at 4,000.
- **The graphics card is not the limit** for scenes of Sponza's size: under a millisecond on the desktop.

## Loading

- **Pictures.** Decoding Sponza's 69 JPEG and PNG pictures in Go took 0.5 s on the desktop, on all cores, and 9.1 s in WebAssembly, which has one thread. On the web the browser decodes pictures (`createImageBitmap`, off the page's thread), through a contract call that takes the file's bytes; the desktop keeps decoding in Go. Textures compressed for the graphics card (KTX2, Basis) are for later.
- **Size.** A web build embeds its assets in the `.wasm` file: Sponza made it 52 MB. 3D assets on the web will be served next to the game and fetched as it reads them, which `golib web` and `golib dist --web` have to learn.
- **Uploading** Sponza to the graphics card took 0.5 s on the desktop and 0.3 to 0.6 s on the web, mipmaps included.

## Screenshots

- **Within a backend, shots repeat byte for byte.** On the desktop, Sponza at frames 1, 30 and 60, shot twice, came out identical.
- **Between backends, they are close.** The desktop's and the web's Sponza differ on 60.4% of their pixels, by more than 8 levels of 255 on 1.16%, worst 163: the edges of cut-out leaves and the mipmaps each driver makes. Tests compare a backend with itself exactly, and the two backends with a tolerance.

## Conventions

- Y is up, right-handed, as in glTF and OpenGL; one unit is a meter; angles in the API are in degrees, as they are in 2D.
- Matrices are column-major, as OpenGL and glTF keep them. Front faces are counterclockwise.
- Color textures are sRGB, lighting is linear, and the 3D pass writes sRGB into the screen, so the 2D drawn over it and the post-processing shaders see what they see today.
